## The idea

Concept line: "A good brand is like a garden." A garden is not a directed experience; it draws you in, is full of diversity, and is designed to change and grow. The brand is "built to bloom": the logo is the sturdy fixed part (like sycamores along a parkway), everything else grows each season.

The one formal device is the Philadelphia city block. William Penn's grid is the logo's module: each plant is illustrated to fit one rectangular block, three plants sprout from the letters PHS (the letters are the roots), and the same block widths become the column grid of every print layout. Where it shows up: logo (3 blocks over 3 letters), illustration library (every flora drawn to a block ratio), layout grids (fig 26 to 28), the grid overlay map paper (fig 23 to 25), the peony symbol drawn on graph paper (fig 57), colour swatches laid as blocks over a meadow (fig 37), the poster collage of 18 equal portrait blocks (fig 48), and the "18 UNIQUE FLORALS" poster (fig 52).

A second device is the tangible: the studio argues a company that deals in tangible things should not be shown in a shiny digital case study, so the case study itself is a book, photographed outdoors in gardens.

What they killed or decided against: the old tree-in-circle logo (too generic, could be a retirement home, never locked up with blocky letters); Philly cliches ("no cheesesteaks or brotherly love, no tropey references"); a purely digital case study; a theme-of-the-year logo for the Flower Show (replaced by a permanent peony symbol with the theme sitting beside it); a restrained palette ("almost too many colours").

## Grid and layout

The grid is the logo. The three plants of the mark are three blocks of equal width; the book and the brand layouts take 3, 4 or 5 column versions where the column width equals the width of one flower block (figs 26 to 28). Wireframe in fig 28 shows 5 columns, with the logo spanning the first three and an image block spanning all five below it, text bars right of the logo. Poster fig 27 follows it: mark top left over three columns, title in the fourth and fifth, rounded image below across all five.

Photos always sit as inset rectangles with rounded corners (figs 27, 35, 55) or as flush blocks (figs 48, 52). Margins are small, about one column gutter. Cropping at the frame edge is routine: type, florals, logo and photos all bleed off (figs 1, 15, 31, 32, 45, 52, 56).

Skeleton flex: the same logo-driven grid produces a member-events cover (portrait), pop-up garden poster, signs, calendar boards and billboards (fig 44). Flower Show posters (figs 49 to 55) have a separate, looser Swiss grid: a single image, one word and one date placed in opposite corners, or the 6 x 3 contact sheet.

## How the case study is staged

1. A flat cream hero slide with the logo and the photos (fig 1).
2. Hook: the book in a hand in a garden (fig 2), then the print suite flat lay (fig 3) with figure labels, then the pin (fig 4) and flip book (fig 5), then a cover hero (fig 6).
3. Joke and offer: the "I've written 50 of these... make it harder" opening and a $60 price, so the case study is also a store.
4. Background: before and after on identical cream pages (figs 7, 8), then "in the wild" documentary photos (figs 9 to 11).
5. 6. Alternation: flat spread on black, then the same spread in hand on an oak table (figs 15/16, 23/25, 38/39, 40/41, 45/47). The viewer reads the page, then feels it.
7. Scale shifts: pin at .625 in, flip book 2 x 3.5 in, book 8.5 x 5.5, poster 18 x 24, billboard (fig 44), and billboard-scale claims for the Flower Show.
8. A false ending ("You probably thought this case study was over") leads into the Flower Show chapter: contact sheet, posters, type specimen, lockups, peony symbol on grid paper.
9. Credits, QR code, and a human sign-off ("love letter to the city"), a list of local makers (photographers, florists, printers, the library on 20th St).
What makes it feel finished: one photoshoot (same table, same sun, same hands) for all in-hand shots; one black ground for all flat spreads; every flat spread captioned by its own headline; the writing is first person, funny and local.

## Transferable principles

1. Derive the grid from the logo. The mark's module sets the column width of every layout (figs 8, 26, 27, 28), so the system hangs together without anyone seeing why.
2. Use one real local reference as the module (the city block). A true, specific origin beats a generic metaphor (figs 23, 24).
3. Make the case study the product: a physical object for a physical brand (figs 2, 3).
4. Show the same page twice: flat and square on black for reading, in a hand under hard sun for touching (figs 15/16, 38/39, 40/41, 45/47).
5. Alternate paper stocks inside one book (aqua, white, orange, pink, translucent) so the turning of the pages makes pacing (figs 13 to 15, 40, 41, 45, 46).
6. One signature finishing detail: an embossed seal sticker on the cover (fig 2). It is the thing a reader touches first and the one object with relief.
7. Label a flat lay like a technical figure: hairline leaders, "fig 1", sizes in inches (fig 3). It gives scale without hands and makes it look archival.
8. Keep the shadows tight on flat lays and let the sun do the work in hand shots (figs 3, 16, 39).
9. Pair a stable core with a growing library: logo fixed, illustrations open to workshops (figs 30, 36, 57).
10. Give sub-brands their own picture, not their own logo: a plant per programme next to the parent mark (figs 29, 32).
11. Show the construction: wireframe beside finished piece (figs 27, 28), grid paper under the symbol (fig 57), the 12-frame storyboard of the animation (fig 12).
12. Let type be rough, large and cropped: ultra condensed words that fill a spread (figs 14, 15, 31, 45, 46), mixed with tiny tabular captions (figs 1, 37, 46).

## What not to copy

- The saturated pink, orange and aqua riot and the "too many colours" stance: Ferry's palette comes from the paintings and its brand is quiet and trusted.
- Cut-paper floral illustration, linocut specks and stamped roughness: that is PHS's hand-made, garden voice; Ferry's illustrations are gouache paintings and its modern layer is clean.
- Ultra-condensed grotesque headlines (Caslon Doric style) and the second display face (Herbus): Ferry Sans only, plus the approved stamp serif and one script on print pieces.
- Gradient-ramp photography backdrops (figs 1, 48 to 55): Ferry has a no-gradients rule.
- Hard dark shadows from direct sun: Ferry keeps shadows quiet.
- The retail and joke tone ("MORE time consuming", $60 store): Ferry's brand is a payments protocol and should be calm.
- A whole rebrand told as a book: copy the staging logic, not the 42-page length.
