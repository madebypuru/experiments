## Anatomy of the back

The back is where the postcard is a postcard. Real backs are almost identical across a century, and that sameness is why they're recognisable instantly.

### 1. The divide

- **A single vertical rule** splits the back: message on the left, address on the right. On most cards it sits at about 50 to 55 percent of the width, slightly right of centre, so the message side is a little smaller.
- The rule rarely runs edge to edge. It stops short of the top and bottom, often leaving room for a heading above and an imprint below.
- **The divide carries small print:** the printer's name runs vertically along it ("C. T. COLORCHROME" set letter by letter down the line), or a logo roundel sits at its foot (the C. T. Chicago seal).

### 2. The heading

- **"POST CARD" or "POSTCARD"** across the top, centred or over the address side. On older cards it's ornamental (Kropp's blackletter "Post Card" in green), on mid-century cards a clean serif ("POST CARD / Address" on the Honolulu card).
- It's the largest type on the back and usually the only display type.
- **Bilingual headings** appear on European cards ("CARTE POSTALE / Correspondance").

### 3. The labels

- Small, spaced caps: "THIS SPACE FOR WRITING MESSAGES", "THIS SPACE FOR ADDRESS ONLY", "For Correspondence", "For Address", "ADDRESS".
- Later and modern cards shorten them to "TO:" and "FROM:".

### 4. The stamp box

- **Top right, always.** A rectangle about the size of a stamp, outlined in a single or double rule, with "PLACE STAMP HERE" inside in two or three lines.
- Variants: a dotted rectangle (Seal Rocks); ornamental corners with tiny arrows and lettering (Kropp's box shows where to place stamps by price); a printed stamp design for prepaid cards.
- On used cards the real stamp covers it, slightly crooked, and the postmark lands half on the stamp and half on the card.

### 5. Address lines

- Three or four horizontal rules on the right half, evenly spaced, starting about a third of the way down. Often slightly indented in steps, the way an address is written.
- Thin, the same colour as the rest of the print.

### 6. Caption and story

- **The front's caption lives on the back,** top left: a small heading ("SEAL ROCKS") and a short paragraph of facts about the place, three to five lines, set in a small sans or serif.
- On some cards the caption sits on the front instead, in the white border under the picture ("BOURNEMOUTH EAST CLIFF FROM PIER").

### 7. Imprints and numbers

- **Publisher line** along the bottom edge: "Published by the Cliff House Gift Shop, San Francisco, California".
- **Card number**, tiny, in a corner or at the top: "CH-2", "12411", "71849", "3C-K1220". Series numbering is everywhere.
- **Printer and place**, often rotated vertically up the left edge: "BLACKWELL WIELANDY, ST. LOUIS. MADE IN U.S.A."
- **Small logos or roundels**: the publisher's mark at the foot of the divide, a tiny pictorial cut (the Cliff House seal illustration, the hula dancer on the Honolulu card).

### 8. Ink and paper

- **One ink.** Nearly every real back is printed in a single colour: blue, green, brown or black. Colour on the back is a modern habit.
- **Card stock:** uncoated, slightly toned cream or grey-white, with visible fibre. Corners rounded on some cards, square on most; the corners wear soft and brown with use.
- **Wear:** foxing spots, a crease, a grubby thumb edge, the ghost of an album corner.

## Anatomy of the front

### Formulas

1. **Picture with a white border** (Bournemouth, Megan Rafferty, Head High). A narrow white or cream frame round the image, often with a one-line caption in the bottom border and a number in the corner. The border reads printed and collected.
2. **Full bleed** (Seal Rocks photo, Lombard Street). The picture to every edge; the caption moves to the back.
3. **Large letters, "Greetings from"** (Memphis, Inlet, Boston, Havana). Huge dimensional letters spelling the place, each letter a window into a scene; "Greetings from" in a script above; the state or country small below. The most iconic and the most cliché.
4. **Brand strip** (Head High). The picture plus a vertical band holding the brand name rotated up the side, with a small subtitle. It turns a scenic card into a product.

### Details

- **Caption in the border:** small spaced caps, left aligned, with a card number right aligned on the same line.
- **The picture:** one place, one moment, usually with depth (foreground, middle, distance) and a lot of sky.
- **Printing look:** halftone dots, linen texture (the classic 1930s to 1950s "linen" cards are printed on embossed card, so colour sits in a fine crosshatch), hand-tinted photos with slightly off colour, chromes with saturated blues.

## Used cards: the details that make them real

- **Handwriting** fills the message side edge to edge, slanted, sometimes running up the margins or across the divide. It's the strongest realism cue of all.
- **The stamp** is placed slightly crooked in the box.
- **The postmark** crosses from the stamp onto the card: a circular date stamp with the town, date and time, and wavy or straight cancel lines (Honolulu: JUN 14, 4 PM, 1960). Slogan cancels appear too ("POST EARLY IN THE DAY").
- **The address** in the same pen, sometimes underlined.
- Messages are short and specific: the weather, where they are, a joke, "see you soon". Signed with a first name.

## Modern series: what designers keep and change

- **Head High** uses the postcard as a product card. Painted coast fronts sit in a cream frame with the name running vertically up the right border. The back is a data card: the wine name, county and year as a heading, a short tasting paragraph, a small table (ALCOHOL, TA, pH, TS, CASES), then logos and a URL. The postcard back becomes a spec sheet without losing the postcard feel.
- **Lombard Street** pairs a screen-print front with a coloured back that keeps the anatomy (divide, address lines, stamp box) in one ink, plus a vertical "POSTCARD" heading.
- **Fieldman** puts the monogram on the front photo and a brand seal and stamps on the back.
- What they keep: the divide, the stamp box, address lines, one ink on the back. What they change: the heading becomes the brand, the caption becomes product copy.

## Presentation patterns

1. **Held in a hand** in front of the place it shows (Leadville against a lake).
2. **Scattered on a surface** (Head High on wood): fronts and backs mixed, overlapping, at angles.
3. **Front over back**, one tilted on the other, on a ground taken from the picture (Megan Rafferty on sky blue, Lombard Street on terracotta).
4. **Close-up of a used back**: the handwriting, stamp and postmark filling the frame.

## AI tells to avoid

- Gibberish handwriting that imitates writing without real words.
- Postmarks with unreadable or nonsense text, stamps with garbled lettering.
- Address lines and divides at random positions, or a stamp box missing.
- Over-aged paper with every effect at once: stains, tears, coffee rings, burnt edges.

## Checklist for a Ferry postcard

1. Standard proportion: 6 × 4 in (152 × 102 mm), landscape, square or slightly rounded corners.
2. Back: one vertical divide just right of centre, "POSTCARD" heading, a stamp box top right, three or four address lines, the front's caption top left, a card number, and a publisher line at the bottom. One ink.
3. Front: one painted place, in one of the formulas above.
4. Card stock: toned, uncoated, visible fibre. Light wear only.
5. When used: a Ferry stamp placed slightly crooked, the postmark crossing onto the card, a short message.
6. Presented as a close-up with soft light, with no added text around it (the rule from the stamp study).

## Open questions for design

1. **Handwriting on the back.** Real postcards are made by handwriting. It would be a fourth voice beside the serif, the script and the sans. Allow a real pen hand on postcards only, or keep the message in the three voices?
2. **Front formula.** Picture with a cream border and caption, full bleed, a brand strip like Head High, or large-letter "Greetings from"?
3. **What the back carries.** A personal message ("Arrived. 41 seconds."), the transfer as data like Head High's spec table (sent, arrived, chains, time, fee), or both?

## Decisions (Nov 11, 2026)

- **Message:** a real pen hand, written, scanned or drawn, and allowed only on the postcard's message side. It's the fourth voice, and it lives nowhere else.
- **Front formula:** design all four (cream border with caption, full bleed, brand strip, large-letter "Greetings from") to start, then pick the one that works best.
- **Back:** both. A short handwritten message on the left, and the transfer's facts as a small table (sent, arrived, chains, time, fee), alongside the standard anatomy.
