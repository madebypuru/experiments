# How the board presents its work

Study of the "2 hour brand exercise" board (27 pins, full resolution), read for one question: how is finished work *presented*, as opposed to designed? Pin numbers match `BOARD_STUDY.md`. No images are copied here.

This is the first input for two skills: a generic one (present any brand's social assets and mockups) and a Ferry one built on top of it.

## The headline finding

**No pin on the board shows a social post inside a phone feed.** The only phone (27, Calendly) is a cropped corner of a device showing an app icon, used as one tile among many. Platform chrome (likes, captions, tab bars, usernames) never appears.

Social assets are presented in one of two ways:
1. **The asset is the post.** 03, 05, 09, 13 and 14 are themselves designed posts, shown edge to edge with nothing around them.
2. **The presentation is a new post about the asset.** Almost every mockup pin is a 4:5 or 3:4 image whose subject is the work, placed in a world. The mockup is content in its own right, not a preview of where the content will run.

So "present the post on a phone" isn't the board's move at all. That's why the phone row and the plain postcard felt flat: they show *where* a thing goes, while the board shows *what it means* and *that it's real*.

## What makes a presentation catch attention

### 1. One hero, cropped by the frame
The main piece is big, often 60 to 80 percent of the frame, and usually cut by an edge:
- The billboard (04) runs off the left side.
- The postcard stack (10) runs off the top and bottom.
- Calendly's "6:25 Run" (27) and the Wing Sang objects (15) are sliced by the frame.
- Jupi's neighbouring cards (09) peek in from both sides.

Cropping says the world carries on past the image. Showing three equal items, whole and centred with margins all round, reads as a catalogue.

### 2. The ground is a place or a material, not a colour
The setting behind the work is almost always somewhere real:
- a lava field (18)
- a faded cotton field (25)
- a Paris book stall (21)
- a street under trees (04)
- a knitted blanket (17)
- a postcard rack and paving stones (24)

When the ground is flat colour, the colour comes from the art: the sky blue behind Megan Rafferty's sky (20), cream behind the cream cards (10). Or it's a strong opposite: navy behind four bright brochures (23), teal under red matchbooks (01).

### 3. Real against made
The hook is often the tension between a photograph and something drawn:
- Lanzarote (18): a photo of the volcano, and a watercolour of the same volcano on top of it.
- The billboard (04): a real croissant used as the drawn woman's handbag, and a real man walking past a drawn woman.
- Chronicle (08): a UI card on a painted ceiling, and a toolbar on an old printed page.
- Calendly (27): a "Scheduling" chip on a photo of friends.

The eye checks which part is real. That moment of checking is the attention.

### 4. The setting adds meaning
The presentation tells a story the asset can't tell on its own:
- Lanzarote: "Postcard from Lanzarote", shown in Lanzarote.
- Fieldman (25): the archive photo, then the thank-you postcard, over the cotton field the brand comes from.
- Odd Sauce (21): the client journey as a route map in a desk calendar, with a real city map and a business card tucked in, at a book stall.

The question each one answers is "where does this live, and why does it matter?"

### 5. A human presence
- Hands (17, 24) are first-person phone photos: natural light, slightly off-level, nail polish, a background out of focus. They look taken, not rendered.
- The passer-by (04) gives scale and a moment in time.

The imperfection is the point. It's proof someone held this.

### 6. Production details sell it
Every physical piece shows how it was made:
- the billboard's paste-up seams and paper wrinkles (04)
- the matchboxes' inner trays (01)
- a paperclip on a curled receipt (01)
- the deckled tooth of the postcard paper (18)
- perforations and rubber-stamp ink spread (16, 22, 25)
- the spiral binding (21)
- the card stock's thick edge in the hand (24)

Smooth, perfect surfaces read as a mockup template. Making-marks read as an object.

### 7. One light source
Light is real and directional: sun through trees on the billboard, one hard shadow per brochure, one soft drop shadow under the postcards. Shadows range from barely there (10) to plainly visible (20, 23), but each image has exactly one light.

### 8. Layering and overlap
Front and back overlap (18, 20, 25), cards stack (10), and objects overlap on a flat lay (01). Overlap gives depth and order. The item in front is the message; the one behind is context.

### 9. Mostly flat or frontal, rarely 3D
Views are top-down, frontal (the billboard, the brochures) or hand point-of-view. Perspective tricks are rare. Realism comes from the ground, the light and the making-marks, not from camera angles.

## The presentation types on the board

| Type | Pins | What it does | When to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| In the wild | 04 | The asset at full scale in a real place, with a passer-by | Posters and outdoor; proves it holds up at size |
| In the hand | 17, 24 | First-person hands holding the piece, natural light, a real place behind | Cards, stamps, tickets, anything pocket-sized |
| Over its origin | 18, 25 | The piece laid over a (sometimes faded) photo of what it depicts or where it comes from | When the art is of a place or thing that exists |
| Stack or cascade | 10, 20 | Several of one format, turned and overlapped, cut by the frame, on a ground sampled from the art | Postcard sets and front/back pairs |
| Flat lay | 01, 15 | A family of objects scattered on a single-colour surface, each turned a little | Showing the brand's world of things in one image |
| Series line-up | 22, 23 | Identical templates in a row; only colour or picture changes; a contrasting ground | Proving it's a system |
| System sheet or bento | 06, 07, 27 | Tiles with tight gutters mixing type crops, UI, photo, pattern and the mark | An identity overview, a launch post |
| Interface over heritage | 08 | A real UI fragment laid on a painting or an old page | Showing a digital product with warmth |
| Carousel peek | 09 | One card centred, the neighbours cut at the edges | Carousels; promises more |
| Process in motion | 26 | Screen recording of the tool making the asset | Reels; showing how the brand system works |
| Editorial post | 03, 05, 13, 14 | The asset is the post, edge to edge | Feed posts that need no frame |

## Why the first Ferry mockups fell short (Nov 12, 2026)

- **Phones in a feed:** platform chrome, three equal items in a row, a flat beige ground, nothing cropped, no story. It shows a post's location, not its meaning.
- **Laptop:** one device, front-on and centred, with nothing around it.
- **Wall and table prints:** closer, but generic. A blank wall and plain linen say nothing about Ferry. There's no hero, the prints are evenly spaced, and there's no real world or human.
- **Close-up print:** the strongest of the six, because one piece fills the frame and shows how it was made (the knife edge). It still has no place.

## Rules the generic skill should start from

1. Present the work as a new post about it, at the platform's own ratio (4:5 for feed, 9:16 only when asked).
2. Pick one hero. Make it big. Let the frame cut it or something next to it.
3. Choose a ground that is a place or material from the brand's world. If it's flat colour, sample it from the art or set it as the art's opposite.
4. Put something real against something made: a photo behind a painting, a real object inside a drawing, a UI chip on a photo.
5. Add one human trace when the object is small: a hand, a passer-by, a cup, a pen.
6. Show the making: seams, paper tooth, perforations, ink spread, folds, a paperclip, binding.
7. One light, one shadow direction, everywhere in the image.
8. Overlap to set order: the message in front, the context behind.
9. Never show platform chrome unless the post is about the platform.
10. Each presentation answers one question: where does this live, what is it for, or how is it made.

# Three designers on X (studied Nov 12, 2026)

@dakshpixelup (Daksh, Pixelup Labs), @kuxshl (Kushal, Oakline) and @socoloffalex (Alex Socoloff). The user named all three as strong presenters. About 100 posts each were read, most-viewed first. Their videos were sampled 8 to 12 frames at a time. Images stay in the scratchpad.

Their brands are mostly AI, fintech, B2B and web3: technical products like Ferry. The question their work answers is how to make a technical brand look expensive, mature and trustworthy in a feed.

## Daksh: the branded stage and the flipbook

- **The presentation has its own frame.** Every Replay post sits on a stage that is itself branded: thin corner crop marks, the logo small at the top, the tagline ("Know what it's worth") small at the bottom, the brand's ornament in the corners, and a field of one brand colour (oxblood, navy, sage, charcoal). The asset sits in the middle. The stage stays fixed while the assets change, so the series reads as one book.
- **The flipbook post.** His most-viewed post (64K views) is a 5.5-second square video. The stage stays fixed and the centre cuts every half second through about a dozen assets: an engraving, a stamp, a poster, a sculpture, a UI card, a data card. It's fast, it loops, and it shows the whole system in one breath. A 24-second 4:5 version gives each asset about 2 seconds.
- **Classical heritage over a modern product.** Engravings, marble sculpture (a Pegasus), gold relief (a lion, a sun god), wax seals, envelopes, stamps and banknote textures, all set against clean serif type and small mono labels. It borrows centuries of trust. His own playbook post says "borrow trust from established signals: banks have trusted the same buildings for centuries."
- **Breaking the frame.** The marble Pegasus overlaps the card's edge and leaves the frame. That one move gives depth.
- **Process as content.** "Sealed, signed, delivered" is a 12-second video: a pencil sketch, then construction lines, then the finished logo. It's paired with dark, quiet product photographs: a folio with a debossed mark, a blue wax seal on dark felt, business cards on charcoal. The light is low, with one source and deep shadow.
- **Platform tricks.** The Bland footer is split across three tall images that line up across X's image gutters into one panorama. Before-and-after pairs ("Sculpted, then flattened") show a mark as a gold sculpture next to its flat version.
- **The words are tiny.** Captions are often one or two words ("more", "Bland", "rejected concepts", "golden artifacts"). Rejected and alternate directions are posted openly; the archive itself is content.

## Kushal: the four-up key-visual grid

- **Four 16:9 images designed for X's four-up grid.** Almost every post is four landscape key visuals that tile perfectly in the feed. There are hardly any physical mockups. The brand presents itself through its own advertising images.
- **Each tile uses a different layout move,** with one palette across all four: a centred emblem, a split screen, a huge type crop, a gold emboss close-up. Same brand, four voices, so the set reads as a system.
- **Heritage imagery made modern.** An engraved eagle, the Statue of Liberty in halftone, a gold embossed knot, Benjamin Franklin off a banknote, Victorian iron gates rendered as dot patterns, a dithered classical bust. All of it sits on paper and grain, with mono spec labels ("01 PRIVATE DEPLOYMENT", "INSTITUTIONAL-GRADE AI"), a large serif headline and a small mark.
- **Texture everywhere:** paper fibre, halftone, riso grain, torn paper collage (Tabs Market), pixelated 3D (Bonzai). Nothing is flat digital colour.
- **Exploration as content.** "Brand exploration", "rejected brand concepts" and "let us know your favourite of these 3 directions" (58K views) invite the audience to take part.
- **The most-viewed post (97K, Axiom)** is cream and navy, engraving plus gold emboss plus serif, labelled like a legal document. That's what "serious" looks like in this set.

## Alex: the brand living in the world

- **Real-world proof in four frames.** His pinned post (200K views, Gather): the wordmark embroidered on the back of a fleece, the logo alone on its colour, a billboard between Manhattan towers, and a lit wall in an airport with silhouetted travellers. In the city shots the brand is at architectural scale.
- **Every format in one place.** Aqua: a lit sign on a tiled wall at night, a cloth-bound book spine with the wordmark, and hands tearing open a letter on a wooden table in warm side light. Fonzi: pin badges on a cap, the mascot inflated in a shop window, a metro poster, a billboard. Each frame shows a different way the brand reaches someone.
- **Brand-system posters at 4:5.** Obside and Jurni are each four 4:5 posters. The layout is constant (wordmark top-left, a two-tone headline top-right, product UI cards floating over a generative pattern that bleeds off the edge), while the colours alternate light and dark. Product truth (real UI numbers) sits over brand art.
- **Paintings as cinema.** The 🕊️ post (75K views) runs the Last Supper as a website's hero, inside a browser frame, with a slow camera move into its details: hands, wine, a face, an eye. Then it pulls back. The painting carries the emotion and the site frame keeps it a product.
- **Tactile mascots.** A knitted version and a keychain version of the mascot on green corduroy, a real material close up.
- **A recurring series format.** "Bad news for Figma, I woke up ☕️" as a running title, and posts with selfies. People follow the person as much as the work.
- **Stamps and posters as deliverables.** Small stamp sets on black, with plenty of empty space.

## What all three share

1. **They present the brand as a system, not a single asset.** A set of four, a flipbook or a series shows range and consistency at once. One lonely mockup never appears.
2. **They design for the platform's grid:** four-up 16:9 on X, 4:5 sets, square loops, and panoramas split across images. The post's own geometry is part of the design.
3. **Heritage borrowed for trust:** engravings, sculpture, gold relief, seals, banknotes, classical paintings, stamps, envelopes. Technical brands borrow the look of institutions that lasted.
4. **Material richness:** paper, grain, halftone, emboss, wax, felt, cloth binding, knitted wool. Close-ups with one light make it tactile and expensive.
5. **Restraint in type.** A large serif or clean sans headline, tiny mono labels, a small mark. Hardly ever more than three type sizes.
6. **Motion is the default.** Most top posts are video: flipbook cuts, process reveals, slow camera moves into a painting, UI in motion. A still is the exception.
7. **Proof of reality** (mostly Alex): the brand at real scale in real places, with people walking past.
8. **Process and the archive are content.** Sketch to final, rejected directions, explorations, "which one?" polls. Showing the thinking builds authority.
9. **Captions are almost empty.** The images carry the post.

## Compared with the board

- The board leans on craft and ephemera in the hand: postcards, matchbooks, a book stall. The X designers lean on institutional heritage and systems: engravings, gold, seals, key-visual sets, the city.
- Both reject the device-feed mockup. Neither shows a post inside a social app.
- The X work adds four things the board doesn't show: **motion (flipbook, process, camera into painting), platform-grid design (four-up and split panoramas), a branded presentation stage, and exploration as content.**
- Both rely on texture, one light, cropping and a real-against-made contrast. Daksh's sculpture breaking the frame and Lanzarote's watercolour over a photo are the same idea.

## Added rules for the generic skill

11. Present a **set**, not a single asset: four tiles, a flipbook, or a numbered series. Vary the layout move across the set, keep the palette and the frame.
12. Give the series a **branded stage**: corner marks, small logo, small tagline, a field of one brand colour. The stage stays; the asset changes.
13. Design to the platform's geometry: four-up 16:9 on X, 4:5 on Instagram, square loops, a panorama split across images.
14. Default to **motion** for hero posts: a fast flipbook through the system (about 0.5 s per asset for a loop, about 2 s for a tour), a process reveal (sketch, construction, final) or a slow camera move into a key image.
15. Borrow trust from a **heritage material** that fits the brand: engraving, relief, seal, stamp, banknote, classical painting. Pair it with clean modern type.
16. Shoot physical pieces **close and tactile** in low, directional light: deboss, wax, cloth, felt, paper fibre.
17. Show the brand at **real scale** at least once: a billboard, a sign, a wall, with a person for scale.
18. Treat **process and rejected directions** as posts in their own right.
19. Keep captions to a few words; the image carries it.

## What this suggests for Ferry (for the Ferry skill, not built yet)

- Ferry's paintings are its heritage material. Alex's camera moving into the Last Supper is the direct model: a slow move into a Ferry gouache, inside a frame, ending on the line "Arrived."
- Daksh's branded stage plus flipbook fits Ferry's system: the stamp, postcard, postmark, ticket and paintings cutting inside one paper-and-ink stage, with the mark top and "Safe passage between chains" at the bottom.
- Kushal's four-up grid fits the 16:9 posters: four tiles per post, each a different Swiss layout, one palette.
- Real-scale proof: Ferry posters at a ferry terminal, a pier noticeboard, on board a ferry. That's real against made, like Lanzarote.
- The stamp and postcard in the hand, close and tactile in low light, with a wax-seal or postmark close-up.

## Client addition, Nov 12: classic base, modern layer

The study above notes heritage "paired with clean modern type" but stops short of the principle behind it. The client stated it: the brands and designers studied (Calendly, Daksh, Kushal, Alex Socoloff) take classic typography, visuals and themes as the base and lay one very modern, high-tech element over it. If the visual is classic, the typography, the pattern or the UI feels new. Calendly does it with crisp UI over a gradient. The balance is what makes the work feel modern and trustworthy at once.

For Ferry: the gouache painting or print object is the classic layer. The modern layer is crisp product UI, a new-feeling pattern, or current type play. Each piece needs both. Do not age the modern layer with grain, bleed or paper texture.
