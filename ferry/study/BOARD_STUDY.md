## What stands out

1. **Tech brands dressed in craft.** Popcorn (telecom), Jupi (decisions software), Chronicle (docs), Calendly (scheduling) and Patternly (a tool) all present software through paintings, grain, frescoes, old paper and soft light. This is exactly the bet in Ferry's brief: a technical product that feels human and calm.
2. **Travel ephemera is the second half of the board.** Stamps, postcards, matchbooks, receipts, maps, tickets and seals make up about half of it. These are objects people keep, and they carry a feeling of time, place and trust.
3. **The real world is always present.** Hands, a billboard on a street, a book stall, a postcard rack, a blanket, a croissant, a real map, a real landscape. Hardly anything floats in a void. Even the bento cards sit in a framed page.
4. **One idea per element.** Every card, stamp, matchbook or panel says one thing. Nothing is crowded.
5. **Texture everywhere.** Grain, paper tooth, letterpress spread, riso dots, watercolour edges. Almost nothing is flat digital colour.
6. **Painting versus photo.** Lanzarote puts a real photo of a place and a watercolour of the same place side by side. That is literally what Paint does, presented as the product.
7. **The journey as a diagram.** Odd Sauce draws a client process as a route from "Starting Point" to "Arrival", with pins at each stop. Ferry's whole voice is departs, crossing, arrives.

## Layouts

Eight layout types repeat across the board.

**1. Flat lay (01, 02, 15).** Objects are scattered on a single-colour surface, each turned a few degrees, with soft shadows. The spacing between objects is roughly even, so it feels arranged, not dropped. No two neighbours share a colour. In 15 the objects run off the edges, which tells you the field continues: it's a pattern, not a composition. Reason: a flat lay feels like looking at someone's table. It's intimate and real.

**2. Bento and cards (07, 09, 27).** Rounded cards with even gutters, each holding one feature. Headlines alternate between the top and bottom of cards, so the grid has rhythm rather than a fixed slot. Imagery bleeds off the card edges and fades into the card colour (07). In Jupi, the neighbouring cards peek in at the sides, promising more. Calendly sits its bento on dark navy gutters so each tile reads as a separate surface. Reason: a card per feature lets a complex product be read one calm piece at a time.

**3. Object in a real scene (04, 17, 18, 21, 24, 25).** The design is shown living in the world: on a billboard, in hands, at a book stall, over a cotton field. Reason: proof. It reads as already made and used, not as a pitch.

**4. Front and back stacked (10, 18, 20, 25).** Two faces of the same object (postcard front and back, photo and painting), overlapped, slightly turned, with a shadow. The top card often hides part of the one below, even part of the wordmark (10). Reason: it shows both the picture and the message. The overlap gives depth and a sense of sequence.

**5. One object in vast space (09, 11, 14).** A single small subject sits on the centre axis, with half the frame or more left empty: sky, cream or blank. The type goes in the empty part. Reason: calm. The emptiness is the message.

**6. Logo as window or supergraphic (03, 04).** The mark is enlarged until it becomes the layout. In Empee the letter is a hole showing the painting. On the billboard the glyph runs out into stripes that leave the frame. Reason: the brand's shape organises the whole image without a logo being "placed".

**7. Series on a template (06, 16, 22, 23).** The same frame repeated, with only the picture and the title changing. The ancient-capitals stamps (22) lock everything: title top left, seal top right, value bottom left, caption bottom right, image centred on a cloud. ivivu (23) changes only the colour per destination. Reason: a series feels collected and trustworthy, and each new item costs nothing to design.

**8. Product UI over heritage material (08, 26, 27).** Real interface pieces (a toolbar, a dropdown, a toggle, sliders, a UI chip) placed on a fresco, an old printed page, a soft sky or a photo. Reason: it shows the product working while keeping the warmth. The UI is honest and small; the art does the emotion.

## Typography (how it's used, not which typeface)

- **Serif to display, sans to explain.** Almost every piece with copy pairs a large serif headline with small sans body (03, 07, 08, 09, 13, 05). The size jump is big, roughly 4 to 6 times.
- **Headlines are short.** Two or three lines, six words or fewer: "Plug out. But stay in loop." "Confidence in every choice." Sentence case, tight leading, nothing shouted.
- **Body is small, muted and centred** in the calm pieces (07, 09): grey rather than black, about 40 to 50 characters per line, two to four lines.
- **Emphasis comes from switching to italic or script, not from bold.** "Gifts for *loved* ones" (13), "*conocernos*", "*tus proyectos*" (05), "Postcard from *Lanzarote*" (18). The change of voice does the work.
- **Script is the human hand.** Used for small things: a place name, a signature ("Brandon Hayden"), "Inspires you", route labels in Odd Sauce. Never for body text.
- **Wide-tracked caps are the functional layer.** URLs, labels, categories, "FROM:", "TO:" (18, 19, 25). Small and quiet, like the print on a ticket.
- **Type follows a circle to become a seal** (01, 06, 25). The seal is a second, small logo.
- **Type sits in the quiet part of the image.** It goes in sky, paper or blank space, almost never in a box. When the image behind is busy, the image is blurred instead (13), or the type gets a soft glass card (08).
- **Numbers are design elements.** "80分" on every stamp (22), "6:25" cropped huge (27), "$3.99" on the bread clip, "1889".
- **Three levels at most.** Headline, body and one label. Never more.
- **Repetition as texture.** "Virtual Virtual Virtual" runs across the top of Café Magnético like a ribbon.

## How elements are arranged

- **Slight rotation** (about 3 to 8 degrees) on physical objects makes them feel placed by hand. Flat UI is never rotated.
- **Soft, consistent shadows.** One light source per image.
- **Overlap means order.** Things in front come first or matter most: the painting over the postcard back, the lighthouse card over the plain one.
- **Cropping at the edges means more.** Patterns, carousels and object fields are cut by the frame on purpose.
- **Symmetry for calm, asymmetry for life.** The calm pieces (09, 14, 11, 03) are centred. The collages are balanced but loose.
- **One focal point.** Even in busy collages, one object is bigger or brighter.
- **The background colour comes from the artwork.** Sky blue behind a sky postcard (20), cream behind cream stamps, a teal surface under red and green matchbooks for contrast.

## How individual elements are designed

- **Grain or noise on nearly every surface** (07, 09, 11, 12, 15, 16). It removes the digital sheen.
- **Small palettes.** Three or four colours per piece, often one warm accent against cool fields: the red flag on the blue mailbox, red pins on a cream map.
- **Hand line plus flat wash** (19): a loose ink drawing with one colour washed under it. That is Paint's ink-and-wash medium.
- **Print artefacts:** perforated stamp edges, rubber-stamp ink spread (01), postmark rings, paper texture, foil numerals (22).
- **Simple surreal metaphors** (09): an ordinary door opening onto sky stands for "confidence". One object, one idea.
- **Depth through focus** (13): a blurred foreground and background give depth without any 3D.
- **Monograms and seals as secondary marks** (06, 25): a circle seal, a single letter, a ghost watermark on the postcard back.

## How features are shown

- **One card is one feature** (07): "No hidden costs", "global roaming", "satellite", "alpha access", each with its own picture.
- **One object is one message** (01): the matchbook carries the tagline, the receipt carries the interview, the bread clip carries a question, the badge names the issue.
- **One panel is one fact** (17): each Oregon panel shows one thing the state is known for (beaver, coffee, crab, lighthouse, waterfall).
- **One pin is one step** (21): a process becomes a route with named stops and a short line under each.
- **A real UI fragment is a feature label** (27): a "Scheduling" chip on a photo of friends, a "Satellite / Cellular" toggle inside a card (07), an image toolbar on a page (08).
- **Before and after** (18, 26): photo becomes painting; image becomes pattern. The product's effect shown as a pair.

## How the final image is presented

- **On a coloured ground sampled from the work** (10, 19, 20), or a dark frame for a system view (23, 27).
- **In hands and real places** (17, 24, 21, 04): proof of physicality.
- **Over a faded photo of its own world** (25: cotton field behind a cotton brand). The background is the brand's origin, pushed back.
- **As a sheet** (06, 22, 27): the whole system at once, evenly spaced, reading like a specimen.
- **As a sequence** (26 is an animation, 10 and 20 are stacks): the work shown as something that changes or arrives.

## Why these choices work

- **Trust through the physical.** Things you can hold (stamps, receipts, postcards, maps) feel accountable. Software brands borrow that trust.
- **Calm through restraint.** Few words, few colours, one idea, lots of space. The brand never raises its voice.
- **Craft signals care.** Grain, hand lines and paint say a person made this slowly. That's the opposite of the "fastest bridge ever" category.
- **Systems make it scale.** Templates (stamps, cards, postcards) turn one good idea into a hundred assets with no new design work.
- **Context makes it believable.** Showing the work in the world answers "what would this feel like?" before anyone asks.

## How the brand pattern forms outside the logo

1. **Extend the logo's geometry** (04: glyph to stripes; 27: pattern strips; 03: letter as window).
2. **A symbol library** (02: birds, hands, sun, frog; 27: the icon set).
3. **An object family** (15, 01): the brand's world of things, repeated as a field.
4. **A format family** (16, 22, 23: stamps; 10, 20, 25: postcards): the frame itself becomes the brand.
5. **A pattern generated from imagery** (26): a tool turns the brand's pictures into the brand's pattern. Paint can do the same.
6. **A route line** (21): a path with stops, reusable anywhere a process is explained.
7. **Seals and monograms** (06, 25): small marks that stamp anything as the brand's.
8. **A repeated word or ribbon** (05).

## Decisions from the study (Nov 11, 2026)

- **Lead world:** a blend. Tech is shown through ephemera: product features appear as tickets, postcards and stamps inside calm bento layouts.
- **Real photos:** allowed for presentation only (hands, piers, desks, places). The brand's own imagery stays painted.
- **Script voice:** yes, in small doses. A captain's hand for place names, signatures and route stops, alongside serif display and sans body.
- **Formats Ferry owns:** ferry tickets and boarding passes (each transfer is a ticket), postcards and stamps (arrival messages from the other shore), and route maps (a crossing drawn with stops).
